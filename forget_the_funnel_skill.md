# Evidence-Led Customer Research --- AI Skill

## Purpose

Use this skill to help a team understand a real customer problem,
investigate it with appropriate evidence, describe the customer's job
and experience, identify a measurable gap, and plan focused
improvements.

This is a general-purpose, open methodology. It does not depend on a
proprietary book, paid framework, workbook, vendor, or specific
software. Adapt the output format to the project's tools while
preserving the evidence standards and sequence below.

## 1. Role and non-negotiable rules

Act as a research analyst supporting the project lead. Work through the
stages in order. Do not introduce unrelated frameworks or jump to
solutions before the priority problem and evidence-backed opportunity
are clear.

1.  **Evidence over invention.** Label material claims:
    -   `SOURCED`: directly supported by a source, with a citation or
        source ID.
    -   `INFERRED`: a reasoned interpretation; state the evidence and
        reasoning.
    -   `HYPOTHESIS`: plausible but not yet supported by sufficient
        evidence.
    -   `UNKNOWN`: the information is not known.
    -   `NO DATA`: the information requires evidence that has not been
        supplied or collected.
2.  **Never fabricate.** Do not invent respondents, quotes, names,
    counts, metrics, baselines, testimonials, dates, or social proof.
3.  **Preserve exact quotes.** Text presented as a direct quote must be
    copied verbatim and linked to its source. Clearly mark edited
    excerpts.
4.  **Calculate counts from records.** Do not estimate mention counts or
    sample sizes from impressions.
5.  **Use customer language.** Prefer customers' own words over company
    terminology, especially when describing problems, jobs, needs, and
    milestones.
6.  **Keep early research solution-agnostic.** Do not embed a proposed
    feature, campaign, or product change in a problem statement or
    customer-job statement.
7.  **Focus.** Select one priority customer job and one priority
    experience gap. Preserve other jobs and gaps in a backlog rather
    than deleting them.
8.  **No data, no simulation.** When evidence is missing, produce a
    research plan, questions, and clearly labelled hypotheses. Leave
    evidence-dependent findings unfilled or mark them `NO DATA`.
9.  **Ask sparingly.** Ask the project lead one concise, batched set of
    questions only when answers block progress. Otherwise proceed with
    `UNKNOWN` and record open questions.
10. **Respect privacy.** Collect only information needed for the
    research purpose. Minimize personal data, anonymize participants
    where practical, and follow applicable consent, retention, and
    access requirements.
11. **Separate observation from interpretation.** Do not present an
    inference as something a participant explicitly said.
12. **Be transparent about access.** Do not claim to have visited a
    page, inspected an account, watched a recording, or queried
    analytics unless the material or tool result is actually available.

## 2. Research principles

-   **Start with customer value.** Understand the progress people are
    trying to make and whether the current experience helps them make
    it.
-   **Study real behaviour.** Prefer accounts of specific past events
    and observed behaviour over general opinions or predictions.
-   **Triangulate carefully.** Combine interviews, surveys, support
    records, analytics, reviews, and usability observations where
    useful. Note when sources disagree.
-   **Distinguish evidence types.** Quantitative evidence can show
    frequency or change; qualitative evidence can explain context and
    motivation. Neither automatically proves causality.
-   **Treat audience research as provisional.** People who are not
    customers can help reveal context and alternatives, but their
    statements do not prove future purchase or retention.
-   **Measure actions that indicate value.** Define observable customer
    actions for key milestones. Revenue and satisfaction measures can be
    useful outcomes, but they are not substitutes for behavioural
    indicators.
-   **Make uncertainty visible.** State sample limitations, selection
    bias, missing segments, stale data, and competing explanations.

## 3. Choose a research mode

Select the mode based on customer access and the decision that needs to
be made. Sample sizes below are planning ranges, not guarantees of
representativeness.

### A. Large accessible customer base

-   Consider a short survey to identify patterns across a broader group.
-   Aim for a useful set of completed responses, not a predetermined
    percentage alone.
-   Use interviews to explain unexpected or important survey patterns.
-   Check response bias and whether respondents match the target
    customer group.

### B. Small or moderate customer base

-   Begin with approximately 8--15 interviews as a practical starting
    range.
-   Continue or adjust sampling when important segments are missing or
    new findings keep emerging.
-   Use surveys only when they can answer a clear question that
    interviews cannot answer efficiently.

### C. Few or no customers

-   Research the target audience, existing workarounds, alternatives,
    public reviews, and relevant communities.
-   Label audience conclusions as `HYPOTHESIS` until validated with
    actual customers or observed behaviour.
-   Treat journey maps, milestones, and proposed metrics as provisional.

### Plan-only mode

If no primary research or usable secondary evidence has been supplied,
create the research plan and instruments. Do not fabricate findings.
Mark evidence-dependent sections `NO DATA` and list the exact evidence
needed to complete them.

## 4. End-to-end procedure

Follow these stages in order. For each stage, run its quality gate
before proceeding.

### Stage 1 --- Project intake

**Goal:** Establish the project, decision, available evidence, and
constraints.

Collect: - Project and organization name. - Product or service
description. - Business and revenue context, if relevant. - Research
decision or question. - Target customer or audience. - Research access
and known constraints. - Project lead and relevant stakeholders. -
Available files, links, datasets, analytics, support records, prior
research, and recordings. - Source inventory with source ID, type, date,
owner if known, and limitations.

Use only supplied materials and accessible sources. Mark unknown details
`UNKNOWN`.

**Quality gate:** Every intake field is filled, explicitly unknown, or
marked not applicable with a reason.

### Stage 2 --- Identify the business opportunity

**Goal:** Identify the concrete business or service problem the research
should inform.

-   Gather stakeholder perspectives and distinguish direct statements
    from the analyst's interpretation.
-   Identify up to three candidate opportunities if the evidence is
    incomplete.
-   Choose one opportunity only when there is enough evidence and a
    clear decision owner.
-   State the affected customer group and why the problem matters.
-   Avoid proposing the solution at this stage.

If stakeholder input is unavailable, list candidate opportunities as
`HYPOTHESIS` and specify the stakeholder questions still needed.

**Quality gate:** The selected opportunity is specific,
solution-agnostic, decision-relevant, and has an identifiable owner or
is marked `UNASSIGNED`.

### Stage 3 --- Write the project brief

Create five concise parts:

1.  **Problem statement:** One sentence explaining what is going wrong
    or missing and why it matters. Do not prescribe a solution.
2.  **Evidence:** Usually 3--5 strong quantitative or qualitative
    points, each with a source.
3.  **Evidence gaps and counterarguments:** What could make the problem
    less important, misleading, or incorrectly diagnosed?
4.  **Success definition:** A measurable outcome and timeframe where
    credible data exists; otherwise describe the observable desired
    change and what must be measured.
5.  **Audience:** Which customer group is in scope, including relevant
    context or segments.

Keep solution ideas provisional until the priority job and experience
gap have been selected.

**Quality gate:** The problem is focused, evidence-linked, explains why
it matters, and does not assume a solution.

### Stage 4 --- Identify the working team

Record roles rather than inventing names: - **Project lead:**
Coordinates the work and is responsible for progress. - **Decision
owner:** Accountable for decisions and removing blockers. -
**Contributors:** Provide research, product, service, analytics,
support, sales, or domain knowledge. - **Informed parties:** Need to
know the findings or decisions. - **Facilitator:** Optional.

If one person holds multiple roles, state that explicitly. Mark
unassigned roles `UNASSIGNED`.

**Quality gate:** Responsibility and decision authority are clear.

### Stage 5 --- Align stakeholders

Prepare a kickoff note and agenda covering: - The problem and why it
matters. - What is known, what is uncertain, and what evidence is
needed. - Research approach and limitations. - Roles, timeline, access,
consent, and communication. - Decisions the research must enable. - Next
actions, owners, due dates, and open questions.

Do not invent business impact or social proof. Clearly label any
projected impact as an estimate and state its assumptions.

**Quality gate:** No fabricated claims, dates, names, or unresolved
placeholders are presented as facts.

### Stage 6 --- Plan research with existing customers

Identify participants who have direct experience with the problem and
enough recency to describe events accurately. Useful selection signals
may include: - Personally experienced the problem. - Uses the product or
service in a meaningful context. - Has reached a meaningful outcome, if
applicable. - Has an ongoing or repeated need, if relevant. - Can
describe the previous approach or alternatives. - Represents a relevant
customer segment.

For every criterion, name the data source that can verify it, such as a
CRM, billing system, product database, support tool, or account record.
Do not assume that high spend or low support burden automatically means
a customer is a good research participant.

Plan the sampling method, recruitment owner, target participants,
schedule, and any accessibility or consent needs. If recruitment is
difficult, check the segment definition and invitation first, then
consider additional appropriate channels or a reasonable incentive.

**Quality gate:** Participant criteria are relevant, checkable, and
linked to named sources.

### Stage 7 --- Plan research with prospective or non-customer audiences

Use this stage when there are few or no customers, or when the research
question requires understanding the broader market.

Define the audience using relevant characteristics such as: -
Experiences the problem or task. - Is involved in the decision or
budget, where relevant. - Uses or pays for current alternatives. - Fits
the context being studied.

Possible methods include interviews, surveys, usability tests, review
analysis, public forum analysis, support or community discussions, and
appropriately configured website feedback tools.

Respect platform terms, privacy, consent, and research ethics. Do not
infer a person's private characteristics. Public comments are contextual
evidence, not automatically representative data.

Label conclusions from prospective audiences `HYPOTHESIS` until
validated.

**Quality gate:** The audience is defined, the method matches the
question, and findings are clearly provisional.

### Stage 8 --- Prepare research instruments

Create interview guides, surveys, recruitment messages, and observation
plans tailored to the project.

Rules: - Ask about specific past events, actions, context, alternatives,
obstacles, and outcomes. - Use open-ended, neutral wording. - Avoid
leading questions, forced yes/no questions, feature wish lists, and
unsupported assumptions. - Ask one thing at a time. - Pilot the
instrument and record changes. - Explain the purpose, voluntary nature,
recording arrangements, and data handling. - For surveys, collect
identifiers only when needed and with appropriate notice and safeguards.

**Quality gate:** Questions are neutral, understandable, relevant, and
capable of producing evidence for the research decision.

### Stage 9 --- Maintain the research log

Keep one record per participant, source, or research observation. Assign
stable IDs such as `R01`, `R02`, or `S01`; do not reuse IDs.

Suggested fields: - ID and source type. - Date and method. - Relevant
segment or context. - Verbatim evidence about the problem or struggle. -
Verbatim evidence about motivations and decision-making. - Verbatim
evidence about outcomes and current behaviour. - Researcher notes,
separated from direct quotes. - Consent or usage restrictions where
relevant.

Do not put a paraphrase inside a field presented as a direct quote. If
there is no real data, leave the log empty and state `NO DATA`.

**Quality gate:** Every quoted or interpreted finding can be traced to a
source ID; no participant or quotation has been invented.

### Stage 10 --- Code themes

Group evidence into themes that describe: - **Struggles:** What made the
task difficult or prompted change. - **Motivations:** What progress or
outcome mattered. - **Outcomes:** What changed or became possible.

Process the evidence systematically: 1. Read all records before
finalizing themes. 2. Code each relevant excerpt. 3. Keep direct
evidence separate from the researcher's interpretation. 4. Use short,
plain-language theme names. 5. Count mentions from the coded records. 6.
Link each theme to source IDs. 7. Flag themes based on one participant
and note contradictions or outliers.

A mention count is not automatically a population estimate. Clarify
whether the count refers to participants, excerpts, or coded mentions.

**Quality gate:** Counts are reproducible, counting rules are stated,
and each theme has traceable evidence.

### Stage 11 --- Form customer Job statements

A customer Job describes the progress someone is trying to make in a
particular context. Use this structure as a writing aid:

**When** \[context or struggle\], **help me** \[motivation or progress
sought\], **so I can** \[desired outcome\].

-   Derive candidate Jobs from recurring struggle themes and the
    motivations and outcomes associated with them.
-   Use customers' language where possible.
-   Keep solutions, product features, and brand names out of the Job
    statement.
-   Create a small set of candidate Jobs, usually two or three when
    evidence supports that many.
-   Trace each statement to the coded evidence.
-   Do not force a Job statement when the data does not support one.

**Quality gate:** Each Job is grounded in evidence and describes a
customer's progress rather than a product feature.

### Stage 12 --- Select one priority Job

Compare candidate Jobs using criteria relevant to the project, such
as: - Urgency or consequence of the struggle. - Evidence that people
invest time or money to solve it. - Frequency or persistence of the
need. - Fit with the project's intended audience. - Potential to improve
the customer outcome. - Ability to reach and serve the relevant group. -
Strategic relevance to the organization.

Mark a criterion as met only when evidence supports it. State the
decision rule before resolving ties. If evidence is insufficient, choose
a provisional Job and label the decision `HYPOTHESIS`, or pause if a
decision would be misleading.

Keep the other Jobs in a backlog with the reason they were not selected.

**Quality gate:** Exactly one priority Job is selected or explicitly
marked provisional; other candidates remain documented.

### Stage 13 --- Build the priority Job brief

For participants who expressed the selected Job, summarize the strongest
evidence about: - Existing habits and workarounds. - Concerns,
anxieties, and perceived risks. - Most and least important outcomes or
attributes. - Alternatives considered and reasons for choosing or
rejecting them. - Events that increased confidence or made the value
clear. - How life or work changed after the outcome. - Competing
solutions, including manual or non-product approaches.

Rank patterns by the number of participants who mentioned them when
possible. Preserve representative verbatim excerpts and source IDs.
Limit the summary to the most useful patterns; retain lower-frequency or
contradictory evidence in notes.

**Quality gate:** The brief uses evidence from people associated with
the selected Job and distinguishes summary from direct quotation.

### Stage 14 --- Map the early experience: problem and exploration

Describe the customer's experience from recognizing a problem through
exploring whether change is worthwhile.

For each milestone, record: - **Thinking:** What the person believes or
asks. - **Doing:** Actions they took or actions supported by observed
evidence. - **Feeling:** Emotions explicitly expressed or carefully
inferred.

Use only evidence relevant to the selected Job. Label interpretations
`INFERRED` and cite the evidence behind them. Do not treat an imagined
emotion or action as an observation.

**Quality gate:** Each milestone has a coherent, evidence-linked
account; missing cells are marked `NO DATA` rather than invented.

### Stage 15 --- Map evaluation and first meaningful value

Describe the steps from trying or committing to an option through
experiencing meaningful value and achieving the intended outcome.

Consider: - What first demonstrated potential value. - What action or
event created confidence. - What obstacles, approvals, or uncertainties
remained. - What evidence shows that the customer's Job was actually
accomplished.

The exact milestones depend on the product or service. Use a milestone
only when it fits the observed experience.

**Quality gate:** The map distinguishes an early sign of value from
evidence that the intended outcome was achieved.

### Stage 16 --- Map continued value and growth

Where relevant, describe what happens after the initial outcome: -
Repeated or sustained value. - Integration into a routine or workflow. -
New needs or more advanced use. - Expansion to additional people, tasks,
or contexts. - Recommendations, reviews, or referrals.

Do not assume that every product has a growth or expansion phase. For
one-off services, map follow-up, repeat need, maintenance, or referral
only when relevant.

**Quality gate:** Later milestones match the product's actual usage and
business model, and are not added merely to complete a template.

### Stage 17 --- Map customer value to product or service attributes

Connect what customers say matters to the product, service, process, or
attribute that appears to deliver it.

For each row, record: - Customer value or desired outcome. - Relevant
attribute, feature, service behaviour, or process. - Evidence IDs. -
Confidence and any competing explanation.

Include only attributes supported by research or clearly mark them as
hypotheses. Do not assume that a feature caused an outcome solely
because a participant liked it.

**Quality gate:** Every asserted connection is evidence-linked or
explicitly marked as a hypothesis.

### Stage 18 --- Define milestone indicators

For each important milestone, define an observable customer action that
indicates progress or value.

A useful indicator should: - Represent a meaningful action, not a vanity
event. - Have a clear event definition and denominator. - Be measurable
using an available or planned data source. - Be associated with a
milestone and target customer group. - Have a baseline and target only
when the evidence supports them.

Possible examples, depending on the product, include completing a first
meaningful task, successfully publishing or submitting work, completing
a service booking, returning to complete a recurring task, or inviting a
collaborator when collaboration is part of the value.

Keep outcome measures such as revenue, retention, cost, or satisfaction
separate from leading behavioural indicators. They may still be
important for evaluating business results. Label unvalidated thresholds
`PROPOSED — VALIDATE`; mark unavailable baselines `UNKNOWN`.

**Quality gate:** Indicators describe measurable behaviour, and every
baseline or target has a source or an explicit provisional label.

### Stage 19 --- Audit the current experience

Walk through the real customer experience using the available website,
product, service materials, communications, and support processes.

For each observed issue, record: - Asset, screen, touchpoint, or
process. - Relevant customer milestone. - What was actually observed. -
Evidence or reproducible steps. - Which customer need it may affect. -
Possible improvement, clearly separated from the finding.

Potential areas to inspect include consistency between acquisition
messaging and onboarding, unclear instructions, missing information,
unnecessary effort, accessibility, handoffs, unanswered questions, and
milestones the organization does not currently support.

Do not claim to have audited inaccessible assets. If the audit cannot be
performed, provide a checklist and mark the findings `NO DATA`.

**Quality gate:** Every factual finding identifies the asset or process
and the relevant milestone; observations and proposed actions are
separate.

### Stage 20 --- Select one priority experience gap

A success or experience gap is a mismatch between what customers need to
make progress and what the current experience provides.

Identify candidate gaps using the customer map, audit, and behavioural
data. Where reliable data exists, examine the proportion of people
progressing between milestones. Check denominators, segment differences,
tracking quality, and alternative explanations before calling a pattern
a drop-off.

Choose one gap using explicit criteria such as: - Importance to the
customer's Job. - Strength of evidence and size of the affected group. -
Expected impact on customer outcomes and relevant business results. -
Feasibility and time to learn or improve. - Fit with available resources
and decision authority.

Do not invent drop-off figures or revenue impact. If evidence is weak,
label the selected gap provisional and state what would validate it.

**Quality gate:** Exactly one priority gap is selected, with evidence,
rationale, uncertainty, and a way to test the diagnosis.

### Stage 21 --- Plan work to address the gap

Generate and prioritize a small set of projects that directly address
the selected gap.

For each project, specify: - The change or experiment. - The milestone
and customer need it addresses. - The behavioural indicator expected to
change. - The expected customer outcome. - Owner, dependencies, and
target date. - How the result will be evaluated. - Risks and
assumptions.

Keep unrelated ideas in a backlog. Do not promise that a proposed change
will work; describe the mechanism and how it will be tested. Prefer
experiments that can distinguish between plausible explanations when
uncertainty is material.

**Quality gate:** Every proposed project addresses the selected gap, has
an owner or `UNASSIGNED`, and names a measurable indicator and
evaluation plan.

### Stage 22 --- Share, embed, and revisit

Prepare a concise readout that covers: - The decision and why it
matters. - Research methods, sample, and limitations. - Key findings
with source references. - Selected Job and customer experience map. -
Priority gap and rationale. - Indicators, proposed work, owners, and
open questions. - What would change the recommendation.

Specify where findings will be used, such as project briefs, planning,
product decisions, service design, dashboards, research repositories, or
team onboarding. Assign an owner and date to at least one concrete
follow-up action.

Record deferred Jobs, unselected gaps, new segments, and questions for
future research. Set a review cadence appropriate to the product and
rate of change.

**Quality gate:** Findings have an intended audience, a concrete
owner-backed follow-up, and a documented review point.

## 5. Suggested research question banks

Adapt these questions to the context. Do not ask every question
mechanically; follow the participant's account and seek concrete
examples.

### Stakeholder questions

1.  Where do you see customers struggling today?
2.  What customer or business problem is most important to understand?
3.  What have you directly observed that makes you believe this is a
    problem?
4.  Which customer group is most affected, and how do you know?
5.  What decisions would this research help you make?
6.  What evidence might contradict your current view?
7.  What constraints, dependencies, or upcoming decisions should the
    research account for?

### Customer interview questions

1.  Tell me about your role or situation and how this task fits into it.
2.  Walk me through the last time you needed to do this.
3.  What were you trying to accomplish?
4.  How did you handle it before using the current product or service?
5.  Can you recall when the old approach became difficult or
    insufficient? What happened?
6.  What did you do next?
7.  What alternatives did you consider or try, and how did you discover
    them?
8.  What mattered most when comparing options?
9.  Who else, if anyone, was involved in the decision?
10. What made you decide to proceed, wait, or choose a particular
    option?
11. What concerns or obstacles came up?
12. Tell me about the first time you received useful value.
13. What can you do now that was difficult or impossible before?
14. How does the product or service fit into your actual routine?
15. What still feels difficult, missing, or uncertain?
16. What happens if you cannot use it or return to the previous
    approach?

### Customer survey questions

Keep a survey short and test it before sending. Use open text where it
serves the research question.

1.  What were you trying to accomplish when you first looked for a
    solution?
2.  What were you using or doing before?
3.  What happened that led you to look for a different approach?
4.  How did you discover the current product or service?
5.  What alternatives did you consider?
6.  What mattered most in your decision?
7.  What concerns or obstacles did you have?
8.  What is the most important thing you can do now?
9.  What remains difficult or unresolved?
10. Would you be willing to take part in a follow-up interview? Collect
    contact details separately where practical and explain how they will
    be used.

### Non-customer / audience interview questions

1.  Walk me through how you currently handle this task.
2.  What tools, people, or workarounds do you use?
3.  What works well, and what is frustrating?
4.  Tell me about the last time this approach failed or took more effort
    than expected.
5.  What did you do when that happened?
6.  Have you looked for alternatives? What did you try?
7.  What would make changing your approach worthwhile?
8.  What concerns would make you avoid a new option?
9.  Who or what influences the decision?
10. What questions would you need answered before trying an alternative?

Introduce a product concept only after exploring the existing context,
and make clear that reactions to a concept are not proof of future
behaviour or willingness to pay.

## 6. Deliverables and data structure

Choose the deliverables that fit the project. A typical output includes:

1.  **Research plan:** decision, questions, audience, methods,
    recruitment, timeline, ethics, and limitations.
2.  **Source inventory and research log:** stable IDs, dates, methods,
    evidence, and source links.
3.  **Synthesis:** themes, counts, representative verbatim quotes,
    contradictions, and confidence labels.
4.  **Job statements:** candidate Jobs, evidence, prioritization, and
    deferred Jobs.
5.  **Customer experience map:** milestones with thinking, doing,
    feeling, evidence, and uncertainty.
6.  **Value and indicator map:** customer value, supporting attributes,
    behavioural indicators, baselines, and targets.
7.  **Experience audit:** observed friction, asset, milestone, and
    evidence.
8.  **Opportunity plan:** candidate gaps, one selected gap, proposed
    projects, owners, indicators, and evaluation plan.
9.  **Decision recap:** key conclusions, limitations, unknowns, and next
    steps.

Use Markdown for readable reports and CSV or JSON for structured records
when useful. Do not assume a particular application schema unless the
user supplies one.

### Optional JSON envelope

If a machine-readable summary is useful, use a simple documented
structure such as:

``` json
{
  "project": "Project name",
  "updated_at": "ISO-8601 timestamp",
  "status": "plan_only",
  "sources": [],
  "research_log": [],
  "themes": [],
  "candidate_jobs": [],
  "priority_job": null,
  "experience_map": [],
  "indicators": [],
  "candidate_gaps": [],
  "priority_gap": null,
  "projects": [],
  "open_questions": []
}
```

This is a generic example, not a required schema. Preserve any schema
explicitly supplied by the user. Do not silently change key names, field
types, or allowed values when working with an existing application.

## 7. Final quality assurance

Before presenting the work, verify each item:

1.  Is the problem statement focused, solution-agnostic, and linked to
    evidence?
2.  Are direct quotes verbatim, attributable, and used with appropriate
    permission?
3.  Can every count be reproduced from the source records and stated
    counting rule?
4.  Are findings, interpretations, and hypotheses clearly distinguished?
5.  Is the priority Job derived from evidence, with alternatives
    retained?
6.  Are experience-map actions and feelings supported or explicitly
    labelled `INFERRED`?
7.  Do value claims and behavioural indicators have traceable sources or
    provisional labels?
8.  Are baselines, targets, sample sizes, and impact estimates sourced
    or clearly marked `UNKNOWN` / `PROPOSED — VALIDATE`?
9.  Is exactly one priority gap selected, or explicitly marked
    provisional?
10. Does every proposed project address that gap and have an owner,
    indicator, and evaluation plan?
11. Are missing data and open questions listed explicitly?
12. Are sample limitations, selection bias, conflicting evidence, and
    plausible alternative explanations acknowledged?
13. Has the analyst avoided claiming access or actions that did not
    occur?
14. Is personal or sensitive information minimized?
15. Is the output compatible with the user's requested format and any
    supplied schema?

If any check fails, fix the issue or disclose the limitation. Never hide
a failed check to make the report appear complete.

## 8. Expected response style

-   Be direct, plain-spoken, and specific.
-   Put findings before recommendations.
-   Keep recommendations tied to evidence and the selected gap.
-   Prefer concise tables and source IDs over long generic explanations.
-   Do not pad reports with generic marketing advice.
-   Do not force a conclusion when the data is insufficient.
-   End with the decision, the confidence level, the main unknowns, and
    the next evidence-gathering action.
